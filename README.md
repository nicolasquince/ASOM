# Frequently Asked Questions

## What is the main objective of the ASOM project?

The primary objective of ASOM is to investigate the feasibility of a low-cost mission architecture for the rapid in-situ exploration of Near-Earth Asteroids. The project focuses on enabling multiple sequential asteroid flybys using a small constellation of identical small simple spacecraft, maximizing scientific return while minimizing mission complexity and cost.

## What makes ASOM different from traditional asteroid missions?

Unlike conventional missions that target a single asteroid over many years, ASOM is designed around a multi-target reconnaissance approach. By leveraging solar-electric propulsion and optimized trajectory planning in near-translunar space, a single spacecraft can encounter many asteroids within one mission lifetime. With a three-spacecraft constellation 100+ asteroids can be visited, enabling broader statistical characterization of the NEA population.

## Is ASOM a real mission or a conceptual study?

ASOM is a fully developed research mission concept validated through high-fidelity simulations and system-level design. While it is not currently scheduled for launch, it demonstrates technical feasibility using existing or near-term technologies and serves as a realistic reference architecture for future small-body exploration missions.






# The Team

## Geza Gyuk

_Senior Director of Astronomy, Adler Planetarium  - Chief Scientist, Orbital Transports_

Dr. Gyuk is Principal Investigator of the Asteroid Surface Observation Mission and Senior Director of Astronomy at the Adler Planetarium in Chicago. An astronomer and planetary scientist with extensive experience in asteroid physical properties and spacecraft mission design, he has led multiple NASA-funded efforts focused on asteroid discovery, characterization, and follow-up observations.

Dr. Gyuk directs the Far Horizons Space Exploration Group at the Adler Planetarium, which for over two decades has developed hands-on spaceflight programs including high-altitude ballooning and satellite missions, and is currently leading the development of NITESat, a CubeSat mission to measure light pollution with unprecedented spatial and spectral resolution.

He teaches Spacecraft Dynamics and Spacecraft Design at the Illinois Institute of Technology and has served as Chief Scientist for Orbital Transports, a space-focused logistics company. Dr. Gyuk is a member of major international time-domain and solar-system collaborations and has served as principal investigator on more than $5 million in competitively awarded research grants, bringing a proven record of translating asteroid science objectives into executable space missions.


## Carlos Serrano Avedillo

_Structural Engineer at Berkey Engineering  - Spacecraft Mechanical and Dynamics Engineer at ASOM. Project Leader_

Carlos is an aerospace engineer holding an MS in Aerospace Engineering from UPC and an MEng in Mechanical and Aerospace Engineering from IIT. The initial concept for this mission originated in his master’s thesis, where the first version of the mission concept was developed. Since then, he has remained responsible for the project components related to spacecraft dynamics, including satellite constellation trajectory design and mission analysis. He is also involved in spacecraft design, particularly in the calculation and sizing of the spacecraft bus and its subsystems, covering all technical aspects outside the scope of the scientific payload.

Alongside this work, Carlos has extensive experience as a private tutor across multiple levels of education, ranging from middle school and high school to university and master’s programs. His tutoring has primarily focused on mathematics, physics, and engineering subjects.

He currently works at a small structural engineering firm, where he participates in the inspection and technical assessment of residential structures. His role includes technical documentation, engineering analysis, and design-related tasks. He is also involved in developing a new line of services based on LiDAR scanning, in which structural data are processed and analyzed using Python-based tools that he has helped develop. In parallel, he contributes to the business side of the firm as marketing manager, working on outreach strategies and partnerships, particularly with home inspectors.

His long-term goal is to work fully in the aerospace sector. He sees this project as a practical step toward that objective, serving as a launch point into the field while allowing him to specialize in orbital mechanics and business development, leveraging new opportunities emerging in the New Space sector.


## Nicolas Quince

_Field Engineer at HP - Spacecraft Mechanical Design Engineer at ASOM_

Nicolas is a Spacecraft Mechanical Design Engineer and graduate aerospace engineer specializing in spacecraft systems design, dynamics, and mission architecture. His technical focus includes spacecraft structure, propulsion systems, mass properties, configuration, and attitude determination and control systems (ADCS), with an emphasis on system-level optimization and multidisciplinary trade studies.

He is currently a member of the Asteroid Surface Observation Mission (ASOM) at the Illinois Institute of Technology, where he led the mechanical and system design of an 80 kg solar-electric spacecraft for multi-asteroid flyby missions. His contributions include subsystem sizing, configuration layout, and development of mission analysis tools to assess propulsion feasibility, navigation errors, and encounter performance.

Nicolas has a hands-on engineering background across aerospace and high-performance industries, including commercial aviation, Formula 1 aerodynamics, fusion energy research, and industrial additive manufacturing. His interests lie in spacecraft mechanical architecture and the coupling between propulsion, structure, and control systems in deep-space missions.


## David Rodriguez

_Graduate Student at Lewis University  - Astrophysicist at ASOM_

David's work focuses on asteroid detectability modeling and the role of non-gravitational forces on the dynamics and orbital evolution of near-Earth objects. As an astronomer with the Asteroid Surface Observation Mission, he contributes to the development of the mission science case through assessing which physical questions are realistically addressable by an in-situ flyby mission coordinated with ground stations.

David is currently a graduate student and teaching assistant at Lewis University. Prior to graduate research, he worked as a research assistant with the BOREALIS lab in Bozeman, Montana. As an undergraduate with BOREALIS, he participated in astrobiology research, focusing on the detection of stratospheric particulate matter.
